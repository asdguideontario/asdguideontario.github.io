Positive reinforcement: celebrate the skill

Positive reinforcement means giving your child something they enjoy — praise, a favourite activity or a small treat — right after they use a skill. It fits naturally with a social story: the story teaches the skill, and reinforcement celebrates it each time your child uses it.

A large review of autism research (National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice, 2020) lists reinforcement as an evidence-based practice for children and youth from birth to age 22.

Your reward charts

Make a separate chart for each behaviour you are working on — for example, one for the bus and one for brushing teeth. Each chart keeps its own goal, stars and reward.

The chart is saved on this device only.

How to do it well

  • Right away. Praise or reward within seconds, so your child connects the reward with what they just did.
  • Say exactly what they did. Not just “Good job!” — try “You put on your headphones on the bus. Great staying calm!”
  • Use what your child loves. Let them choose the reward, and keep a few choices ready in case one stops being exciting.
  • Start small. Begin with only a few stars to earn, so success comes quickly.
  • Never take stars away. Stars are earned for the skill — they are not removed for other behaviour.
  • Fade slowly. Always give praise with each star. As the skill becomes a habit, give stars less often and let praise do more of the work.
  • Be consistent. Share the chart with your child's teacher or support staff, so everyone celebrates the same skill.

Link it to your social story

Use the coaching sentence from your story as the goal on the chart. For example, story: “I can put on my headphones on the bus.” Chart goal: “Headphones on when the bus is loud.” Read the story together, then give a star each time your child uses the skill.
