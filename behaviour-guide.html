Understanding behaviour: a parent guide

All behaviour is communication. This guide helps you understand why your child does what they do, which behaviours need support and which need acceptance, and what helps — in a way that respects how autistic children experience the world.

A large review of autism research (National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice, 2020) lists functional behaviour assessment, functional communication training, antecedent-based supports, reinforcement and visual supports among the practices with good evidence.

Why does my child do that? The function of behaviour

Behaviour usually has a purpose: it gets your child something they need, or helps them avoid something that feels too hard. Professionals call this the function of the behaviour. Most behaviour serves one or more of four functions:

🙌To get attention or connection

Your child wants someone to notice, play, comfort or help them.

Example: Shouting when a parent is on the phone.

What helps: Give lots of attention when things are going well, and teach a better way to ask — a tap on the arm, a word or a picture card.

🚪To escape or avoid something

A task, noise, place or demand feels too hard or too much.

Example: Throwing the pencil when homework starts.

What helps: Make the task smaller or easier, use a First–Then board, and teach your child how to ask for help or a break.

🧸To get an item or activity

Your child wants something and does not yet have a better way to ask for it.

Example: Crying at the store for a toy.

What helps: Teach a quick way to ask, make waiting clear with a timer or picture, and practise waiting for short times first.

🌀Sensory: it feels good or calming

The behaviour itself feels good, helps your child calm down, or blocks out uncomfortable sounds, lights or touch.

Example: Rocking, humming or covering ears in a noisy room.

What helps: Change the environment (less noise, headphones, a quiet space) and offer safe ways to get the same feeling.

The same behaviour can have different reasons for different children, or at different times. That is why finding the “why” matters more than the behaviour itself.

Be a behaviour detective: the A-B-C log

For one or two weeks, write down what happens before, during and after the behaviour. Patterns will point to the reason.

  1. A — Antecedent: what happened just before? Where, when, who was there, and what was asked?
  2. B — Behaviour: what exactly did your child do? Describe it so anyone could picture it.
  3. C — Consequence: what happened right after? What did your child get, or get out of?

Example: A — asked to turn off the tablet → B — screamed and threw it → C — tablet time ended later. Possible reason: to keep the activity, or to avoid the change.

Bring your notes to your child's therapist, behaviour analyst (BCBA) or doctor. This is the first step of a functional behaviour assessment (FBA).

Stimming: what it is and when to help

Stimming (self-stimulatory behaviour) means repeated movements, sounds or actions — like hand-flapping, rocking, spinning, humming, repeating words or lining things up. Autistic adults describe stimming as a way to calm down, cope with strong feelings or sensory overload, focus, or show joy.

Most stimming is healthy and does not need to stop. Stopping harmless stims can make stress worse.

When to help

  • When it causes injury — for example head-banging, biting or picking skin.
  • When it is unsafe in that place, or seriously stops learning or daily life.

How to help

  • Look for the trigger: is your child overwhelmed, bored, anxious or excited?
  • Offer a safe stim that gives a similar feeling — a chewy necklace instead of biting, a squeeze ball instead of hitting, firm pressure from a cushion instead of head-banging.
  • Change the environment: less noise, a quiet corner, movement breaks.
  • Ask an occupational therapist (OT) for a sensory plan.

Common behaviours and what helps

🌋Meltdowns

A meltdown is not a tantrum. It happens when a child is overwhelmed and loses control. It is not on purpose and is not used to get something.

What helps: Keep everyone safe, lower noise and talking, give space and wait. Talk about it later, when calm. Learn the early signs and help before overload.

🏃Wandering or running off

Many autistic children try to leave safe places. In one large survey, almost half had tried at least once after age 4.

What helps: Secure doors, gates and pools; use ID (a bracelet or tag); tell neighbours and school; teach “stop” and “wait”; use a social story about staying safe.

🤕Hitting, biting or self-injury

Often a sign of pain, overload, or having no other way to communicate.

What helps: Check for pain or illness first (ears, teeth, constipation, poor sleep). Keep everyone safe, use an A-B-C log to find the reason, and ask for professional help early.

🔁Insisting on routines

Sameness feels safe and predictable. Sudden changes can feel scary.

What helps: Warn before changes, use picture schedules and First–Then boards, and practise small planned changes.

💬Repeating words (echolalia)

Repeating words or lines from shows is often a way to communicate, understand or calm down.

What helps: Respond to the meaning behind the words, and model the words your child can use.

🍽️Eating only a few foods

Taste, texture and smell can feel much stronger to many autistic children.

What helps: Offer new foods next to safe foods, without pressure. Talk to your doctor or a dietitian if you are worried about growth or nutrition.

😴Sleep problems

Trouble falling or staying asleep is common in autistic children.

What helps: Keep a calm picture bedtime routine and limit screens before bed. Ask your doctor if problems continue.

👂Not following instructions

Your child may not have understood, may need more time to process, or may be overwhelmed.

What helps: Get attention first, use few words, show a picture, wait (count to 10 in your head), then help.

Cheat sheet: autism-informed behaviour support

Keep this on the fridge or share it with everyone who cares for your child.

Do thisInstead of this
✅ Ask: what is this behaviour telling me?✖ Asking only: how do I stop it?
✅ Plan ahead for hard times — warnings, pictures, breaks✖ Waiting for problems and then reacting
✅ Teach a better way to meet the same need✖ Only punishing the behaviour
✅ Praise or reward the behaviour you want, right away✖ Only noticing problems
✅ Use few words, pictures and a calm voice✖ Long talks during a meltdown
✅ Accept harmless stims✖ Stopping stims that cause no harm
✅ Check for pain, hunger, tiredness and sensory overload✖ Assuming your child is being naughty
✅ Use the same words and steps with every caregiver✖ Different rules at home and at school
✅ Offer choices when you can✖ All-or-nothing demands
✅ Look after yourself — ask for help and take breaks✖ Trying to do it all alone

When to get more help

Ask for professional help if behaviour causes injury, is getting worse, or stops your child or family from daily life. A behaviour analyst (BCBA), psychologist, occupational therapist or your doctor can do a functional behaviour assessment and make a plan with you. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.
