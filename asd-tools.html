Social Stories: A Step-by-Step Guide

A social story is a short, simple story that helps a child understand a situation — what will happen, how other people may feel, and what they can do. This page shows parents, teachers and staff how to make one, using words, pictures, or both, and where to find free ready-made stories.

    What is a social story?

    Social stories were developed by Carol Gray in 1990. They describe a situation, skill or idea in a calm, accurate way, so a child knows what to expect. A good social story:

    • Has one clear goal — one situation or one skill.
    • Has a title, a short introduction, a middle with details, and an ending that sums it up.
    • Uses “I” or the child's name — never “you.”
    • Has a positive, patient tone and words that are literally true.
    • Answers who, what, where, when, why and how.
    • Describes more than it tells the child what to do.
    • Fits the child's age, reading level, interests and attention span — and is updated when needed.

    Source: Carol Gray's criteria as summarized by Life Skills Advocate; ABA Resources.

    When can a social story help?

    • Getting ready for an event — a field trip, a haircut or a doctor's visit.
    • Coping with a change in routine or something unexpected, like a fire drill.
    • Understanding how other people might feel or react.
    • Learning an expected behaviour — for example, what to do after finishing work.
    • Self-care skills, such as brushing teeth or getting dressed.

    Social stories can be used at any age. They work best when they are made for one child and one situation.

    How to write a social story: 8 steps

    1. Choose one situation

      Pick one situation your child finds hard, or one new event coming up. Write from your child's point of view: “I go to the store” or “Adam goes to the store” — not “You go to the store.”

    2. Mix your sentence types

      Use at least two describing sentences for every coaching sentence (a sentence that gently suggests what to do). For example:

      • Describing: I ride the bus to school.
      • Feelings: Sometimes the bus is loud and crowded, and I feel upset.
      • Coaching: I can put on my headphones and listen to music.
      • Positive ending: Staying calm on the bus is good for me.

    3. Write the story

      Keep sentences short, with one idea per page. Give it a title, a beginning, a middle and an end.

    4. Add pictures

      Put each picture above its sentence. Use clear pictures — real photos of your child and the real place work best. Avoid busy or abstract drawings.

    5. Make copies

      Print one copy for home and one for school or child care. Laminating helps it last. For a group, print it large or show it on a screen.

    6. Read it together at a calm time

      Introduce the story when your child is calm and ready — not in the middle of a hard moment. Read it several times, let your child read along if they can, and connect it to real experiences.

    7. Practise and praise

      Act the story out together (role-play). Praise your child when they use the skill. Keep the story handy to reread, and update it when things change.

      ⭐ Make a reward chart to celebrate the skill

    8. Bonus: make it together

      When you can, let your child help choose the photos and words. Ask open questions like “What happens next?” Children often like stories they helped make.

    Source: Life Skills Advocate — How to Write a Social Story (A Step-by-Step Guide).

    Words, pictures, or both?

    Word-based story

    Best for children who read or listen well. Use short, simple sentences and the mix of two describing sentences for each coaching sentence. A few pictures help keep attention.

    Visual story

    Best for younger children, early readers and children who are nonverbal. Use mostly pictures and photos, with one or two words per page — a story can even be pictures only. Point to the pictures as you read, and use photos of your child whenever you can.

    Many families use both: a picture on top and a short sentence underneath.

    Source: Life Skills Advocate (adapting social stories for students who are nonverbal).

    Make your own with Google Slides

    You can make a personal social story for free with Google Slides (or PowerPoint), using real photos of your child and the places they go.

    1. Open Google Slides and start a blank presentation. Name it after the story — for example, “Going to the dentist.”
    2. Use one slide for each page of the story.
    3. Add a photo at the top of each slide (Insert → Image). Take photos of the real place, people and objects.
    4. Type one or two short sentences under the photo, in large, easy-to-read text.
    5. Read it on a tablet or phone, or print it (File → Print) and staple it into a small book.
    6. Share it with your child's teacher or support staff, so everyone uses the same words.

    Photos of your child are personal. Share the story only with people who support your child.

    Fill-in-the-blank starter

    Copy this outline and fill in the blanks. Change the words to fit your child and the situation.

    • Title: ____________ (for example, “Going to the Dentist”)
    • Describing: My name is ____. Sometimes I ____.
    • Feelings: When I ____, I may feel ____.
    • Other people: Other people may feel ____ when ____.
    • Coaching: I can try to ____.
    • Positive ending: When I ____, I feel proud.

    Use storybooks as social stories

    Picture books from the school or public library can start a conversation about a topic such as friendship or fitting in. Some books that may be suitable:

    • The Berenstain Bears and the In-Crowd
    • The Berenstain Bears and the Trouble with Friends
    • Groundhog's Dilemma
    • The Cow That Went Oink
    • How to Be Cooler Than Cool

    Read together, then talk: What happened? How did the character feel? What could they do next time?
