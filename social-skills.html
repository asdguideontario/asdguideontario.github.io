Social skills: help with everyday situations

Many autistic children find some everyday social situations confusing, tiring or stressful. For each common situation below you will find why it can be hard, what helps, and words to practise — plus buttons to make a social story or start a reward chart for it.

A large review of autism research (National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice, 2020) lists social skills training, social stories, modelling and video modelling, peer-based support, visual supports and reinforcement among the practices with good evidence.

First: respect your child's way of connecting

The aim is to help your child feel comfortable, safe and understood with other people — not to make them act less autistic. Do not force eye contact; many autistic people listen better without it. Flapping, rocking or talking about a favourite topic are fine unless they cause harm. Work on one skill at a time, and allow breaks.

How to teach any social skill: 4 steps

  1. Tell

    Explain the skill simply, before the situation happens. A social story works well here.

  2. Show

    Show what to do: act it out yourself, use toys, or watch a short video of someone doing it.

  3. Practise

    Role-play at home when your child is calm. Keep it short and fun. Then try it in the real place, starting with an easy version.

  4. Praise

    Praise or reward right away each time your child tries — use a star chart for the skill you are practising.

These four steps follow behavioural skills training (instructions, modelling, practice and feedback), an approach used in many studies of teaching social skills.

Common situations and what helps

👋Greeting people

Why it can be hard: Knowing what to say, when to say it and where to look can be hard. Looking at faces can feel uncomfortable.

What helps:

  • Offer choices: say hi, wave or give a high five — they all count.
  • Practise with family and familiar people first. Looking near the face, or not looking, is fine.

Words to practise: “Hi, Sam!” or a wave

⏳Waiting and taking turns

Why it can be hard: Time is hard to see, so waiting can feel endless. It may not be clear when the turn will come back.

What helps:

  • Use a visual timer, or a “first … then …” card (first wait, then swing).
  • Practise with short waits and turn-taking games at home, then make the waits a little longer.

Words to practise: “My turn, then your turn.”

🧸Joining play

Why it can be hard: Starting is the hardest part. Other children's games can have unspoken rules that change quickly.

What helps:

  • Teach one simple opening line and practise it. Watching for a minute first is a good strategy.
  • Start with one friend and a structured activity, such as blocks, a ball game or a board game.

Words to practise: “Can I play too?”

🤲Sharing

Why it can be hard: Favourite items feel safe and predictable. It may not be clear that a shared item will come back.

What helps:

  • Use a timer so your child knows when the item comes back.
  • Put very special items away before visitors come, and practise sharing with less-loved toys.

Words to practise: “You can have it when the timer beeps.”

🎲Losing a game

Why it can be hard: Losing can come as a surprise, the rules may feel unfair, and the feelings are big.

What helps:

  • Play short, low-pressure games at home and show calm losing yourself: “Oh well, maybe next time.”
  • Praise being a good sport, not winning. Agree on a calm-down plan before the game starts.

Words to practise: “Good game!”

🙋Asking for help

Why it can be hard: Your child may not know how to ask, or may wait until they are very upset.

What helps:

  • Teach one simple way to ask: words, a sign, or a “help” card your child can hand to an adult.
  • Practise during easy tasks, and respond quickly and kindly every time your child asks.

Words to practise: “Help, please.”

🎉Noisy or busy places

Why it can be hard: Noise, lights and crowds at parties, malls or school assemblies can overwhelm the senses and lead to a meltdown.

What helps:

  • Prepare ahead with a social story or photos of the place. Bring headphones or a comfort item.
  • Agree on a break signal and a quiet spot. It is fine to arrive early or leave early.

Words to practise: “I need a break.”

💬Back-and-forth conversation

Why it can be hard: Knowing when to talk, when to listen and how to stay on the topic is hard. A favourite topic can take over.

What helps:

  • Practise “ask one question back”, using conversation cards or passing a ball to show whose turn it is.
  • Use your child's favourite topic as a bridge to friends who share the same interest.

Words to practise: “What do you like?”

🫧Personal space

Why it can be hard: Body cues are hard to read, so it can be hard to know how close is too close.

What helps:

  • Teach a simple rule: “Stand about an arm's length away.”
  • Practise with a hula hoop or tape on the floor, and praise good spacing.

Words to practise: “I stand an arm away.”

🔄Changes in plans

Why it can be hard: Routines make the day predictable. Sudden changes can feel scary.

What helps:

  • Use a picture schedule and give a warning before changes: “5 more minutes, then …”
  • Add a “change” card to the schedule, and practise small, planned changes.

Words to practise: “Plans can change. That's OK.”

Share it with the school

Tell your child's teacher, educational assistant (EA) or school team what works, so everyone uses the same words, pictures and rewards. You can ask for social skills goals in your child's Individual Education Plan (IEP).

Need more help?

Ask your child's therapist or school team about social skills groups or peer programs. Part A of the main guide lists Toronto agencies — call them and ask what social skills support they offer.
